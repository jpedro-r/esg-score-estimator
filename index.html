ESG Score Estimator

An ESG score rates a company's environmental, social and governance performance from 0 to 100. Scores are published mostly for large listed companies, and well after their financial statements. This tool estimates the score a company would be likely to receive from its country, its industry and a few figures in its annual accounts, so that a company without a rating can get a first indication of where it stands.

By João Pedro Gaier Ribeiro and Megha Patnaik. Everything is calculated in your browser; nothing you type is sent anywhere.

How the estimate is made, and how well it works

This page summarises the research behind the estimator, following the structure of the master's thesis it grew out of, Predicting ESG Ratings: A comparative analysis of Machine Learning models.

1. Introduction

In 2015 the United Nations set out the Sustainable Development Goals, and sustainability has since become a mainstream concern for companies and investors. Companies act on it through corporate social responsibility, and rating providers summarise the result in ESG scores. Because sustainability reporting follows many standards with few legal obligations, providers need time to gather the data, and scores are published later than financial results. This work asks whether a company's ESG score can be predicted from information that is available earlier: its financial statements, its industry and its country.

2. Literature review

Four strands of research motivate the question. Studies since the 1970s mostly find a positive link between corporate social performance and financial performance, largely through reputation. Socially engaged companies also appear more resilient, with a lower probability of default and less financial volatility. Sustainability is associated with innovation and competitive advantage, and with faster adjustment towards target leverage. Finally, recent papers predict ESG scores directly from financial data with machine learning, using random forests, gradient boosting and neural networks, and report that balance-sheet information explains a meaningful share of the scores.

3. Data

The scores are ESG scores from Refinitiv (now LSEG), which combine several hundred environmental, social and governance measures into one number from 0 to 100, weighted by industry and benchmarked against peers. The thesis sample covers 355 companies in 22 countries and 88 industries, each observed every year from 2015 to 2019, for 1,775 observations.

The predictors are financial ratios taken from annual accounts (return on equity, turnover ratios, working capital to assets, debt to assets, dividend ratios, earnings per share) together with country, industry, whether the company publishes ESG disclosure, and its controversy score. Skewed ratios were log-transformed, and variables were chosen to keep correlations between pairs low. ESG scores cluster clearly by country, which an analysis of variance confirms.

4. Methodology

Four models were compared, each trained on 70% of the observations and tested on the remaining 30%. A fifth was added after the thesis.

  • Generalised linear mixed model. A linear model built for panel data, with random effects for country and year. It is the most interpretable and shows how scores cluster by country.
  • Random forest. Many decision trees, each grown on a random sample of the data, with predictions averaged. Industries were aggregated into 10 broad sectors.
  • XGBoost. Trees built in sequence, each correcting the errors of the previous ones. The learning rate was tuned to 0.1.
  • Support vector machine. A kernel method that fits a smooth non-linear function. Cost and gamma were tuned to 10 and 0.1.
  • Neural network. Added after the thesis: a small feed-forward network that learns its own representation of country and industry, trained several times and averaged.

5. Empirical analysis

Models are compared on mean squared error, its root (RMSE), and R², the share of variation in scores explained.

Accuracy rises with the model's ability to capture non-linear relationships. The linear model performs worst; the two tree-based methods are close to each other; the support vector machine and the neural network perform best.

6. Conclusion

Financial data carries real information about ESG scores, and models that handle non-linear relationships and mixed quantitative and qualitative inputs predict them fairly well without any sustainability information. Country of operation sets a baseline for the score, which makes it hard to separate a company's own sustainability performance from the effect of national laws and regulation.

7. From the thesis to the estimator

The estimator on this site extends the thesis. The data was enlarged to 9,380 company-years from 1,436 companies in 27 countries and 123 industries, covering fiscal years 2015–2019 and 2021–2023. Inputs a company cannot easily supply were removed (controversy score, earnings per share, dividend ratios, market value). Countries were grouped using the IMF classification of advanced and emerging economies by continent, industries were made finer, and the model was trained to cope with missing figures.
